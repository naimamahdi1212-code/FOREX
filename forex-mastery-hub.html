Foundations

What is the Forex market?

Forex — short for "foreign exchange" — is the global marketplace where currencies are traded against one another. It's the largest and most liquid financial market in the world, and it never fully closes on weekdays because it follows the sun across trading hubs worldwide.

How it actually works

Currencies are always traded in pairs — you're simultaneously buying one currency and selling another. Trading happens over-the-counter (OTC) through a global network of banks, brokers, and financial institutions rather than on a single centralized exchange, which is why prices can differ slightly between providers.

Who participates

Central banks, commercial banks, hedge funds, corporations moving money across borders, and individual retail traders all take part. Each group trades for different reasons — from managing national monetary policy to simply speculating on price movement — which is part of what makes the market so dynamic.

4Major trading sessions

The four major trading sessions

Price behavior shifts depending on which financial centers are active. Understanding session overlaps helps explain why volatility rises and falls throughout the day.

22:00 – 07:00 UTC

Sydney

Opens the trading day. Generally quieter, with lower volatility as major desks in Europe and the US are closed.

00:00 – 09:00 UTC

Tokyo

Asia's main session. Yen pairs see the most activity, with moderate volatility and tighter ranges than London or New York.

08:00 – 17:00 UTC

London

The busiest session by volume. Most of the day's directional moves and trend formation tend to originate here.

13:00 – 22:00 UTC

New York

Overlaps with London for several hours — historically the most liquid and volatile window of the trading day.

Core Vocabulary

Forex basics, explained simply

Every trader needs to speak the language of the market before reading a single chart. These are the terms you'll see everywhere on MT5 and in every strategy on this page.

01 · PAIR

Currency Pairs

Currencies are always quoted relative to another, e.g. EUR/USD. You're never buying a currency in isolation — you're expressing a view on one against the other.

EUR/USD = Euro vs US Dollar
02 · BASE

Base Currency

The first currency listed in a pair. It represents the currency you are buying or selling — the price tells you how much of the quote currency it's worth.

In EUR/USD, EUR is the base
03 · QUOTE

Quote Currency

The second currency in the pair — the one used to price the base currency. It's sometimes called the "counter currency."

In EUR/USD, USD is the quote
04 · BID/ASK

Bid and Ask

The bid is the price you can sell at; the ask is the price you can buy at. The ask is always slightly higher than the bid.

Bid 1.0845 / Ask 1.0847
05 · SPREAD

Spread

The gap between bid and ask. It's effectively the cost of entering a trade and varies by broker, pair, and market conditions.

0.2 pip spread on EUR/USD
06 · PIP

Pips

The standard unit of price movement — usually the fourth decimal place for most pairs, or the second for JPY pairs.

1.0847 → 1.0857 = 10 pips
07 · LOT

Lots

A lot is a standardized trade size. A standard lot is 100,000 units of the base currency; mini and micro lots let you trade smaller sizes.

1 lot = 100,000 · 0.01 = micro
08 · LEVERAGE

Leverage

Borrowed capital that lets you control a larger position than your account balance alone would allow. It magnifies both gains and losses equally.

1:100 leverage example
09 · MARGIN

Margin

The portion of your account set aside as collateral to open a leveraged position. Trading beyond your available margin can trigger a margin call.

Required margin ≠ risk taken

Platform Walkthrough

Your complete MT5 beginner guide

MetaTrader 5 is the platform you'll use to watch the market, place trades, and manage risk. Follow this sequence once, in order, before you touch a live account.

1

Download MT5

Get the official MetaTrader 5 application from your broker's website or the platform's official distribution channel — always verify you're using a legitimate source.

2

Install MT5

Run the installer on desktop, or download the mobile app. The setup wizard walks you through the standard installation in a couple of minutes.

3

Create a broker account

Register with a regulated broker that offers MT5 access. This is separate from the platform itself — the broker provides your trading account and pricing.

4

Open MT5

Launch the application. On first run you'll be prompted to log in or open a demo account directly from the welcome screen.

5

Log in to your account

Enter the login number, password, and server name provided by your broker. Double-check the server — it's a common first-login mistake.

6

Understand the dashboard

Get familiar with the Market Watch panel, Navigator, chart workspace, and Terminal window before doing anything else — this is your entire cockpit.

7

Add currency pairs

Right-click inside Market Watch and choose "Show All," or add specific pairs individually so you can track the ones relevant to you.

8

Open charts

Double-click any pair in Market Watch to open its chart. Explore timeframes — from 1-minute to monthly — using the toolbar along the top.

9

Place your first trade

Open the "New Order" window (F9), select a volume, and choose Buy or Sell. Practice this repeatedly on a demo account before it becomes second nature.

10

Set a stop loss

Define the price level where a losing trade automatically closes. This is the single most important habit for protecting your account.

11

Set a take profit

Define the price level where a winning trade automatically closes, so you don't need to watch the screen to lock in a planned target.

12

Monitor open positions

Use the Terminal window's "Trade" tab to track floating profit or loss, and the "History" tab to review everything you've closed.

13

Practice on a demo account

Spend real time here — a demo account uses simulated funds so you can learn the platform and test ideas with zero financial risk.

14

Transition from demo to live

Only move to a live account once your process is consistent on demo. Start small, and expect the experience to feel different once real money is involved.

What changes going live

  • Emotional discipline becomes real — fear and greed weren't present on demo.
  • Execution can differ slightly due to real slippage and liquidity.
  • Risk management stops being theoretical — every stop loss matters.

Starting small, on purpose

  • Trade minimum position sizes for your first live weeks, regardless of confidence.
  • Judge yourself on process — following your plan — not on short-term outcomes.
  • Keep a simple trading journal to track decisions, not just profit and loss.
Reading the Chart

Technical analysis fundamentals

Technical analysis is the study of price and chart patterns to understand market behavior. It doesn't predict the future — it helps you react to what the market is actually doing.

Support & Resistance

Price levels where buying or selling pressure has historically stepped in, often causing price to pause, reverse, or break through.

Trend Lines

Diagonal lines connecting swing highs or lows that visualize the current direction and speed of the market.

Market Structure

The pattern of higher highs and higher lows (uptrend) or lower highs and lower lows (downtrend) that frames every other decision.

Breakouts

When price moves decisively beyond a support or resistance level, often signaling the start of a new directional move.

Pullbacks

A temporary move against the prevailing trend before it resumes — often used as a lower-risk entry point by trend traders.

Candlestick Patterns

Visual price formations built from open, high, low, and close data that can hint at potential reversals or continuations.

Popular Forex strategies

No single strategy works in every condition. Understanding how each one behaves — and when it tends to struggle — matters more than picking a "best" one.

The Most Important Section

Risk management comes first

Every strategy above only matters if your account survives long enough to use it. Risk management is what separates deliberate trading from gambling.

Position sizing

Decide how large a trade should be based on your account size and the distance to your stop loss — never a fixed lot size out of habit.

Stop losses

A predefined exit point that limits how much a single trade can cost you. Treat it as non-negotiable, set at the moment you enter.

Risk-to-reward ratio

Compare what you stand to lose against what you stand to gain before entering. A favorable ratio can offset a lower win rate.

Capital preservation

Protecting what you have takes priority over growing it. Small, consistent losses are recoverable; large ones often aren't.

Trading psychology

Fear, greed, and impatience influence decisions more than most beginners expect. A written plan helps remove emotion from the moment.

Avoiding overtrading

More trades isn't more opportunity — it's more exposure to cost and error. Quality of setups matters more than frequency.

Learn From Others

Trading mistakes to avoid

Most of these aren't knowledge problems — they're discipline problems. Recognizing them in the moment is half the battle.

Revenge trading

Jumping back into the market immediately after a loss to "win it back," usually with a bigger size and less of a plan.

Overleveraging

Using excessive leverage relative to account size, turning ordinary volatility into an outsized risk of ruin.

Ignoring stop losses

Moving or removing a stop loss mid-trade to "give it more room," which quietly removes your risk control entirely.

Emotional trading

Letting fear, excitement, or boredom drive entries and exits instead of a predefined plan.

Lack of a trading plan

Entering trades without defined criteria for entry, exit, size, or risk — reacting to the market instead of executing a plan.

Chasing trades

Entering late after a big move has already happened, out of fear of missing out rather than a valid setup.
